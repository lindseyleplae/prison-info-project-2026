Prisons and jails use a lot of specialized words and abbreviations. This page explains the ones you are most likely to run into, in plain language.

Different states — and sometimes different facilities in the same state — use different words for the same thing, or the same word for slightly different things. Treat these as general explanations, and confirm the specifics with the state or facility.

## Words for a person in prison

**Inmate / offender / resident / incarcerated person** — Different systems use different words for a person in custody. Many corrections departments now say "resident" or "incarcerated person"; others still use "inmate" or "offender." They all refer to the same thing.

**Committed name** — The legal name a person is booked and held under. Mail and money usually must use this exact name, along with the person's ID number, even if the person goes by another name.

**DOC number / ID number** — A unique number assigned to each person in a state's custody. It is called different things in different states — for example an SID, OPUS, ADC, or booking number. You almost always need it to look someone up, send mail, or send money.

## Custody, housing, and status

**Classification** — The process a prison uses to decide a person's custody level and where they are housed, based on factors like sentence length and history.

**Custody level / security level** — How restrictive a person's housing is — commonly minimum, medium, close, or maximum. It can affect visiting, such as whether visits are contact or non-contact.

**General population (GP)** — The main housing where most people live, as opposed to restrictive or special-purpose units.

**Restrictive housing** — Also called segregation, administrative segregation, "the SHU" (Security Housing Unit), or solitary. Housing where a person is separated from general population and kept in a cell for most of the day. Visiting and phone access are usually limited.

**Protective custody (PC)** — Separate housing for people who need protection from others.

**Intake / reception / diagnostic** — The first stage after sentencing, where a person is processed, evaluated, and classified before being assigned to a facility. Visiting is often restricted during this period.

**Death row / under a death sentence** — Housing for people sentenced to death, in the states that have the death penalty. The rules there differ from general population.

## Types of facility

**Prison vs. jail** — A prison, run by a state or the federal government, holds people serving longer sentences. A jail, usually run by a county or city, holds people awaiting trial or serving short sentences. This site covers state prisons; county jails have their own separate rules.

**State, federal, and private prisons** — State prisons are run by a state corrections department; federal prisons by the Federal Bureau of Prisons; private prisons by a company (such as CoreCivic or the GEO Group) under contract. This site covers state facilities.

**Unit / institution / facility** — Different words for an individual prison. Some states, such as Texas, say "unit."

**Reception / diagnostic center** — The facility, or the part of one, where intake and classification happen.

**Work camp / work release / community corrections / transitional center** — Lower-security settings, usually for people near the end of a sentence, sometimes allowing work outside. The rules differ from a main prison.

**Annex** — A separate housing area attached to a main prison, sometimes with its own visiting schedule.

## Visiting

**Approved visitor list** — The list of people cleared to visit someone. You usually have to apply and pass a background check before your first visit, and the person in prison typically starts the process. See [Visiting Basics](/guides/visiting-basics/).

**Contact visit** — A visit in the same room, usually allowing a brief hug at the start and end.

**Non-contact visit** — A visit through glass or a barrier, often by phone handset, with no physical contact.

**Video visit / video visitation** — A visit by video, either from a kiosk inside the facility or, in some systems, from home through an app. It is not the same as a free video call, and it usually costs money.

**Extended or family visit** — A longer, private visit that a few states offer to people who qualify. Sometimes called a conjugal visit.

**Special visit** — A one-time visit approved outside the normal schedule, sometimes for family traveling a long distance.

**Dress code** — Rules about what visitors may wear. Being dressed out of code is one of the most common reasons visitors are turned away, so check the rules before traveling.

**Duty warden / warden** — The staff member in charge. The duty warden often makes the final decision on whether a visit or a visitor is allowed on a given day.

## Staying in touch: mail, phone, and money

**Commissary** — The prison store, where a person can buy food, hygiene items, and supplies using money in their account.

**Trust account / inmate account** — The account where a person's money is held. Family can add money to it, and the person spends it at commissary or on phone and messaging. See [Sending Money](/guides/sending-money/).

**Money order** — A prepaid paper payment. Many facilities accept money orders for a person's account, mailed to a specific address. It is often the cheapest way to send money.

**E-messaging / electronic messaging** — Short digital messages, like email, sent through a vendor's app or website, usually for a per-message fee. It is not the same as regular mail.

**Off-site mail scanning / digital mail** — A growing number of states send incoming personal mail to an outside company that scans it, and the person reads a copy on a tablet or kiosk instead of getting the original. Legal mail usually still goes to the facility. See [Staying in Touch](/guides/staying-in-touch/).

**Legal mail / privileged mail** — Mail from an attorney or a court. It gets special handling and is usually opened only in the person's presence.

**Publisher-only / approved-vendor** — A rule that books, magazines, or packages must be shipped directly by an approved store or publisher, rather than sent by family.

**Tablet / kiosk** — Devices people use for calls, messages, media, and reading scanned mail. They are provided by a vendor, usually for a fee.

**Vendors (Securus, ViaPath / GTL, JPay, ICSolutions, Access Corrections, TextBehind, ConnectNetwork)** — Private companies that states hire to run phones, video, messaging, tablets, or money transfers. Which company a facility uses determines how you set up and pay for calls and deposits.

## Costs and care

**Money transfer fee** — The fee a vendor charges to add money to a person's account. It varies by how much you send and which method you use, which is why a mailed money order is often the cheapest option.

**Co-pay** — A small charge for a medical visit in some systems. Care is generally not denied for inability to pay.

## Finding someone and getting answers

**Inmate locator / offender search** — A state's online tool for finding where a person is held and their ID number. Each state has its own, and results can lag behind reality, so confirm before traveling.

**Grievance** — The formal complaint process a person inside uses to report a problem.

**Ombudsman** — An independent office, in some states, that reviews complaints about prison conditions or treatment.

**Detainer / hold** — A request from another agency to keep custody of a person after their release date, which can affect where they go next.

**Good time / earned time** — Days subtracted from a sentence for good behavior or completing programs, which can move up a release date.
